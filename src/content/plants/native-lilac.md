*Hardenbergia violacea*, or the Native Lilac, is one of those plants that has successfully made its way into home gardens and streetscapes — and with good reason! Its rich leaves and tumbledown purple flowers make for a sweet sight equally in modern native designs or ramshackle cottage gardens.

*Violacea* means violet, in case you couldn’t guess, and Hardenbergia is obviously named after the Countess Franziska von Hardenberg.

Aristocratic by name only, *Hardenbergia violacea* actually comes from a very hard-working family: the Fabaceae. As we’ve learnt in previous lessons, most plants in the Fabaceae, or legume family, house Rhizobia bacteria in nodules in their roots. The Rhizobia draws down atmospheric nitrogen for the plants, and the plant feeds the Rhizobia. This is, you guessed it, a classic symbiotic relationship. Their special relationship helps them to survive and colonise difficult areas, while also preparing it for successive plants.

Hardly a one-trick wonder, the Native Lilac flowers from winter to late spring, making it one of few pollination options for bees and insects in the cooler months. It naturally climbs and trails between and over plants, creating habitat for birds and insects.

The Native Lilac has a widespread distribution in South Australia, along with Tasmania, Victoria, New South Wales and Queensland. In spite of this and its ubiquity in native gardens, it is actually near-threatened on the Fleurieu Peninsula.

Here in the Forktree Nursery we grow at least a few hundred *Hardenbergia violacea* each year. Their hard seed coats are naturally damaged by fire, allowing water to enter and germination to begin. To simulate this we soak our seeds in hot water before sowing. Our Native Lilacs go to various restoration projects across the Fleurieu, and we always put as many as we can on the property. We often plant them next to prickly shrubs to make a safe habitat for birds. Right now they are in full bloom, rambling away underneath our remnant Pink Gums.
